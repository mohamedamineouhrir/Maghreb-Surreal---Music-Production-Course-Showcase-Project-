PHASE 01 ~40 Hours
01

THEORY &
KEYBOARD.

Understanding sound before you ever touch a machine.

01.1

The Language of Emotion

We begin with the question nobody asks: why does a minor chord feel sad? You will learn major and minor scales not as formulas, but as emotional tools. We break down intervals, the chromatic scale, and diatonic harmony — building the vocabulary needed to understand any piece of music on earth.

  • Major & minor scale construction
  • Intervals: whole steps, half steps, tritones
  • The chromatic scale and Western harmony
  • Reading and writing basic notation
01.2

Chord Progressions & Tension

Chords are not static objects — they create movement. This module covers triads, seventh chords, and the emotional geometry of chord progressions. We study the I–IV–V–I framework, modal mixture, and borrowed chords. Every week, we deconstruct a real song's MIDI architecture together.

  • Triads, 7ths, and extended chords (9th, 11th, 13th)
  • The Nashville Number System
  • Tension and resolution: leading tones, suspensions
  • Weekly song deconstruction (MIDI analysis)
01.3

Keyboard Fluency

Theory without physical application is incomplete. This parallel module runs alongside the theoretical sessions: you will practice scales, chord voicings, and inversions on a real keyboard. The objective is not to become a pianist — it is to become fluent enough that a keyboard becomes your primary compositional tool.

  • Proper technique, posture, and hand position
  • Two-hand coordination and basic voicings
  • Playing chord progressions in multiple keys
  • Weekly performance: playing a full song by ear
01.4

Rhythm, Groove & Song Structure

Harmony is only one dimension. We cover the mechanics of rhythm: time signatures, syncopation, polyrhythm, and groove. Then we zoom out and study how great records are architecturally structured — verse, chorus, bridge, intro, outro, and the emotional logic that governs when each section appears. By the end of this module, you can reverse-engineer the structure of any song.

  • Time signatures: 4/4, 3/4, 6/8, and compound meters
  • Syncopation, swing, and the mechanics of groove
  • Standard and non-standard song architectures
  • Analyzing records: from pop to cinematic scoring
PHASE 02 ~60 Hours
02

DAWs & SOUND
ARCHITECTURE.

From passive listener to active sonic sculptor.

02.1

Inside the DAW

We demystify the Digital Audio Workstation completely. What is audio routing? What is a track, a bus, a send, a return? We cover the fundamentals of signal flow in Ableton Live and FL Studio — the two DAWs we specialize in — while acknowledging the alternatives: Logic Pro, Pro Tools, Reaper. You will feel completely at home before writing a single note.

  • Signal flow: audio vs. MIDI, inputs, outputs, routing
  • Ableton Live: Session View, Arrangement View, Clips
  • FL Studio: Channel Rack, Playlist, Mixer architecture
  • DAW comparisons: Logic, Pro Tools, Reaper, Bitwig
02.2

Audio Manipulation

Before synthesis, we manipulate what already exists. You will take your own keyboard recordings and learn to warp them into entirely new instruments. Chopping, reversing, pitch-shifting, time-stretching, and half-timing are not effects — they are compositional tools. We will build full pieces using only the audio you recorded in Phase 01.

  • Warping and time-stretching in Ableton
  • Chop and rearrange: building loops from raw audio
  • Pitch manipulation: formant shifting, transposition
  • Reverse, half-time, and stutter techniques
02.3

The Character of Classic Machines

Before we explain how synthesis works mathematically, we want you to feel the difference. This is the bonus section: we listen to the CS-80, the Minimoog Model D, the Oberheim OB-X, the Roland Juno-106. We study why these machines were defining instruments for entire genres. You will learn to identify a synthesizer by ear before you learn to operate one.

  • The Yamaha CS-80: 8-voice poly, Blade Runner sound
  • The Minimoog Model D: bass dominance, filter character
  • The Roland Juno-106: chorus-driven pads and atmospheres
  • The Oberheim OB-X: string-like pads and warm detuning
02.4

Synthesis Foundations

Now the math. Subtractive synthesis is the foundation of every synth you will ever use. We cover the oscillator, the filter, and the amplifier — the three fundamental building blocks — and then the envelope (ADSR) and the LFO that shape them. Through Arturia's V Collection, every concept is heard immediately on the very machines we studied in the previous module.

  • Oscillator waveforms: sine, saw, square, triangle, noise
  • Filters: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, resonance
  • ADSR: Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release — shaping a sound over time
  • LFOs: vibrato, tremolo, filter wobble, rhythmic modulation
02.5

Timbre & Sound Design

Timbre is why two instruments playing the same note sound completely different. This module goes deep: we study harmonic content, overtones, and why certain sounds feel warm, bright, aggressive, or hollow. Then we apply this knowledge to sound design — no more searching for presets by adjectives. You will learn to build the exact texture you hear in your head from scratch.

  • Harmonic series and overtone structures
  • Frequency tags: bright, dark, thick, thin, hollow, nasal
  • Layering and stacking: how to build rich, complex timbres
  • Designing pads, leads, basses, and percussive textures from init
02.6

Sampling & Creative Manipulation

Sampling is its own art form. We cover the full creative pipeline: finding source material, chopping it into playable instruments, and repitching it into entirely new melodic ideas. We study how Kanye West, J Dilla, and Pete Rock built their sonic identities through sampling, and then we do the same with your own source material.

  • Sampling workflow: finding, clearing, and manipulating sources
  • Chopping in Simpler (Ableton) and FL Studio's Slicex
  • Repitching samples into melodic instruments
  • Texture sampling: field recordings, room tone, analog warmth

03.1

Gain Staging & Headroom

The most ignored principle in bedroom production. Gain staging is the act of managing levels at every stage of the signal chain to ensure your mix has headroom, clarity, and translation across different playback systems. We unlearn the habit of cranking every fader to 0 dB and rebuild a proper gain structure from scratch.

  • What headroom is and why it matters for mastering
  • Setting proper input and output levels at each stage
  • Metering: RMS, Peak, LUFS — reading levels correctly
  • Clipping prevention vs. analog saturation
03.2

EQ: Carving Space

EQ is a surgical tool, not a tone knob. We cover parametric EQ in depth: high-pass filtering, shelf EQ, notch cuts, and presence boosts. We learn the specific frequency ranges that define each element — the weight of a kick, the body of a vocal, the air of a cymbal — and then we carve with intention rather than boosting randomly.

  • Frequency map: sub, bass, low-mid, mid, high-mid, air
  • High-pass filtering: cleaning up every track, not just bass
  • Surgical cuts vs. broad strokes: when each is appropriate
  • Tonal EQ: using Pultec-style EQ for character and warmth
03.3

Compression & Dynamics

Compression is not volume — it is the shaping of dynamics over time. We start with the fundamentals (threshold, ratio, attack, release, knee) and immediately apply them to drums, bass, and vocals. We study classic hardware emulations: the CLA-76 for fast, aggressive transient control, the CLA-2A for smooth, program-dependent leveling.

  • Threshold, ratio, attack, release, makeup gain
  • VCA vs. Opto vs. FET compression: character differences
  • Parallel compression: adding punch without killing dynamics
  • Sidechain compression: creating groove-locked movement
03.4

Low-End Theory

The low-end is where most mixes fall apart. We dedicate a full module to understanding why — mono compatibility, phase cancellation between kick and bass, and the frequency masking that destroys clarity below 200 Hz. We develop a system for low-end management that ensures your track sounds correct on car speakers, earbuds, and studio monitors equally.

  • Mono compatibility: why the low-end must be mono
  • Kick and bass relationship: sidechain, EQ carving, layering
  • Sub-bass translation across different playback systems
  • Reference tracks: using professional mixes as calibration tools
03.5

Space & Depth: Reverb & Delay

Space is how you place sounds in a three-dimensional field. Reverb and delay are not effects added at the end — they are architectural tools. We study how to create depth, width, and distance: a close, dry vocal in the front, a wide synth pad in the back, a drum room that feels like a physical space. We also study the abuse of reverb, which is equally important to avoid.

  • Room vs. Hall vs. Plate vs. Spring reverb: character and use cases
  • Pre-delay: separating the dry source from the reverb tail
  • Stereo width: mid-side processing and haas effect
  • Delay as rhythm: synced delays, ping-pong, tape echo
03.6

Mix Translation & Referencing

A mix is only as good as the number of systems it sounds great on. This final module is about translation: testing your work across multiple playback environments, using professional reference tracks to calibrate your ear, and building a final checklist before export. We also cover the fundamentals of mastering — loudness normalization, limiting, and what to expect from a mastering engineer.

  • Checking your mix on multiple systems: monitors, headphones, phone speakers
  • A/B referencing workflow with professional records
  • Preparing your mix for mastering: stem exports, headroom targets
  • Limiting and LUFS targets for streaming platforms
